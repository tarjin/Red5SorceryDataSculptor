User research practice

A broad research toolkit — from the usability lab to the user’s workplace.

Before product ownership became the centre of my work, I spent years in hands-on UX research at Microsoft and then Global Market Research at Adobe, backed by formal experimental research in Human Factors. The toolkit ranged from controlled interface studies and prototyping to field research, messaging studies, and large-sample market research, and I also taught research techniques directly to development teams.

Explore the full research toolkit · 11 methods
01

Lab usability testing

Traditional moderated usability studies, including think-aloud / talk-aloud protocols while participants completed realistic tasks.

02

Benchmarking & A/B testing

Controlled comparative interface testing and benchmarking used to evaluate competing designs and interaction approaches.

03

Cognitive walkthroughs

Structured cognitive walkthroughs used to evaluate proposed workflows, features, interactions, and specifications before implementation.

04

Pluralistic cognitive walkthroughs

Collaborative walkthroughs that brought multiple perspectives into the evaluation of interface concepts and task flows.

05

Contextual & field research

Site visits, contextual inquiry, and ethnographic research focused on understanding real work practices in the environments where they occurred.

06

Focus groups

Moderated group research used to explore user needs, reactions, expectations, terminology, and emerging product questions.

07

Surveys & large-sample market research

Quantitative survey research conducted with large-sample market research vendors, including Harris Interactive, alongside specialized user-research consultants.

08

Net Promoter Score research

At ACD Systems, designed and conducted Net Promoter Score (NPS) survey research with actual ACDSee Windows users to understand why they would — or would not — recommend the product, turning recommendation drivers and detractors into product evidence.

09

Paper & interactive prototyping

Used low-fidelity paper prototypes and higher-fidelity interactive prototypes to make design alternatives tangible before implementation. During my Adobe years, I built an interactive Macromedia Director simulation of the proposed Adobe–Google Toolbar distribution experience. The work turned a not-yet-built installation flow into something the partnership team could test under tight deadlines, giving negotiators evidence about how the experience would actually feel to Adobe users. Adobe publicly described the multi-year Google agreement as expected to represent significant revenue over a period of years; Reader 8 later became one of the free Adobe download experiences through which the Google Toolbar was offered.

10

Experimental Human Factors research

My academic research included controlled experimental interface testing published in Human Factors: “Designing an Interface to Optimize Reading with Small Display Windows” ↗.

11

Research workshops for development teams

At Microsoft, delivered workshops to the SharePoint development team on using cognitive walkthroughs while designing features and writing specifications.

The through-line: research was never an isolated deliverable. The point was to make product decisions better — by observing users, testing assumptions, exposing workflow friction, and giving designers, developers, and product teams evidence they could act on.
Product history

From studying software to owning what ships.

I started by observing how people used complex software and turning that evidence into design guidance. Over time, the responsibility moved closer to the product itself: research, then feature and workflow decisions, then release ownership, regulated certification, and now founder-led delivery.

01 · Research

Understand the real workflow.

Microsoft and Adobe taught me to start with observed behaviour, controlled research, and the difference between what users say and what they actually do.

02 · Product ownership

Turn evidence into a release.

At ACD Systems and Medusa, I moved into prioritization, workflow design, requirements, validation, launch readiness, and externally governed product delivery.

03 · Founder-led delivery

Own the whole decision chain.

AI in practice

Daily AI use for complex software development and documentation.

I work extensively with ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini inside a structured, human-led product-development process. AI contributes across the lifecycle; final product decisions and acceptance remain mine.

AI is embedded in the work — requirements, implementation support, critique, QA, documentation, evaluation, and verification.

The value is not simply using multiple models. It is assigning clear responsibilities, preserving decision evidence, reconciling competing input, and keeping one accountable human responsible for the product.

1. Problem & workflow analysisFrame the user problem, operational constraints and intended outcome.
2. Requirements & architecture supportDevelop requirements, contracts, edge cases and implementation options.
3. Implementation supportUse AI to support development while maintaining product-level control.
4. Adversarial QASeparate critique from authorship to expose contradictions and failure modes.
5. DocumentationCreate explainable records of decisions, product behavior and user guidance.
6. Human acceptanceReconcile evidence, make the final decision, and accept or refuse the release.
